Bachelor thesis · paper

Brain–LLM feature correspondence

Language-model features can predict both held-out model activity and human fMRI responses. This project asks the harder question: do those targets rely on the same features in the same way?

Current status

The bachelor thesis is complete. A more focused paper develops the same central idea with a tighter claim, stronger controls, and a deliberately conservative conclusion: Can We Detect Shared Features Between Language Models and the Brain?

01 · Evolution

One research idea, developed in two forms.

The thesis and the paper belong to the same research program: testing whether sparse language-model features provide a meaningful bridge between model activity and human responses to spoken stories. They differ in scope and purpose.

Bachelor thesis · broad and exploratory

Build the research system and investigate the space.

The thesis was the more open-ended version of the work. It included dataset and stimulus selection, exploratory fMRI analysis, transcript and timing infrastructure, SAE feature discovery, qualitative inspection, and focused feature probing.

  • Compared candidate naturalistic fMRI datasets before selecting the paired Shapes stories.
  • Built auditable feature evidence from transcript activations, Dolma contexts, judge summaries, and local alignment records.
  • Tested individual feature hypotheses with synthetic probes, counterfactual edits, and optional steering interventions.
  • Explored complementary model-side, sensitivity, and behavioral-benchmark analyses.
What the thesis found

In the cleaned 48-run shapesphysical analysis, the three completed model analyses converged at about r = .17 on the brain side with a small positive R² ≈ .034. Feature-importance correlations looked high (.909–.961), but sample-level RSA remained low (.019–.067). The exploratory result suggested overlap in feature salience, not matching moment-by-moment representational geometry.

Paper · focused and polished

Turn the strongest idea into one defensible test.

The paper trims the exploratory branches and centers the cleanest question: after a sparse feature bank transfers to a second description, do the brain and the model depend on those coordinates in the same order?

  • Freezes feature selection on the source description before either target or the held-out description is examined.
  • Measures reliance through held-out conditional and grouped ablation, not coefficient magnitude.
  • Tests correspondence against temporal and feature-identity nulls.
  • Uses semi-synthetic calibration to state exactly how strong a negative conclusion the experiment can support.
What the paper found

The source-selected basis still transferred, although BOLD prediction was weak (r = .068–.092). But the held-out reliance rankings did not agree: at 256 features, the mean across twelve model-direction conditions was ρ = −.056 and no better than the fully retuned temporal null (p = .890). This weighs against strong correspondence while leaving moderate correspondence unresolved.

Why the result changed

The thesis result came from an exploratory, cleaned single-story comparison of fitted feature importance. The paper raises the bar: features are selected on one description, transferred unchanged to the other, and judged by the loss caused by removing them in held-out data. It also controls for shared story timing and correlated features. Under that stricter test, the earlier apparent salience agreement does not generalize. That difference is the intellectual progression of the project, not an attempt to hide the first result.

02 · Question

Prediction is not the same as correspondence.

The tempting claim

If one sparse feature bank predicts activity in a language model and in the brain, it is tempting to call that shared representation.

The actual test

Freeze the features selected on one spoken description, transfer them unchanged to a second description of the same events, and compare which features each target truly depends on.

03 · Pipeline

From spoken stories to a calibrated correspondence test.

  1. 01

    Select a defensible paired stimulus

    Exploratory analysis compared candidate naturalistic fMRI datasets and their timing quality. The final design uses shapesphysical and shapessocial: different spoken descriptions of the same animation, heard by the same 59 participants.

  2. 02

    Build a shared time axis

    Audio and transcripts are normalized, long recordings are chunked for Whisper word timing, and Gentle/Kaldi forced alignment refines word and phoneme boundaries. Words and model-specific tokens are then mapped to the scanner's 1.5-second TR grid.

  3. 03

    Construct clean brain targets

    fMRIPrep outputs are summarized into 200 Schaefer cortical parcels. Motion and physiological nuisance terms are regressed out; high-motion, high-DVARS, and non-steady-state response rows are censored while the complete timing grid remains intact.

  4. 04

    Extract model states and sparse features

    Each timed transcript is processed in causal context windows. Hidden states and residual-stream SAE activations are retained across six depth-spaced layers, then aggregated from tokens to TRs alongside the model's final hidden-state target.

  5. 05

    Discover, inspect, and probe features

    The thesis ranks transcript-relevant features by temporal activity and preserves their strongest tokens, spans, sentences, corpus contexts, and judge summaries. Selected features can then be tested with synthetic examples, counterfactual edits, and steering as an auditable interpretability track.

  6. 06

    Freeze the source-only feature bank

    For the paper's decisive test, feature identities are chosen from one description without looking at BOLD, model targets, or the second description. Nested banks from 64 to 1,024 features are carried unchanged into the paired telling.

  7. 07

    Fit matched ridge models

    The same sparse coordinates predict two different outcomes: lagged cortical BOLD and the model's unlagged final hidden state. Contiguous temporal folds, nested penalty selection, training-only scaling, and a purge around BOLD test blocks reduce leakage.

  8. 08

    Ablate, compare, and calibrate

    Each feature—or correlated feature group—is removed only in held-out data to measure the loss in predictive performance. Brain and model reliance rankings are compared in both transfer directions, against fully retuned temporal nulls, identity nulls, robustness sweeps, and a semi-synthetic power analysis.

04 · Brain data

What the fMRI signal actually looks like.

The analysis does not treat “the brain” as one score. It begins with quality-controlled time series, preserves the full story timeline through censoring, and models responses across a 200-parcel cortical atlas.

How to read this

The colorful cortex is not a claim that an SAE feature is a biological mechanism. These maps establish the measured BOLD target, its reliability, and the anatomical distribution of held-out prediction. The correspondence question is tested separately through feature-removal profiles.

05 · Scale

Enough variation to test the idea across systems.

Data

  • 59 participants from Narratives fMRI
  • 200 Schaefer cortical parcels
  • Two spoken descriptions of the same animation

Models and controls

  • Six SAE systems across three model families
  • Model sizes from 1B to 12B parameters
  • Temporal and feature-identity nulls
06 · Result

Transfer survives; importance structure does not.

r ≈ .07–.09

The frozen feature basis weakly predicts held-out BOLD responses.

ρ = −.056

The observed BOLD–model feature-profile agreement is no better than the retuned temporal null (p = .89).

07 · Interpretation

A narrower, more defensible conclusion.

What the work supports

A feature basis selected from one description contains signal that transfers to another description for both targets.

What it does not establish

The evidence does not support a strong claim that brain responses and model hidden states organize their reliance on those features similarly. Power analysis bounds strong correspondence without claiming total absence.
